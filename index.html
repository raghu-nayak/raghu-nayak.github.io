Calculators

Three tools for money over long horizons, by Raghu Nayak.

Each of these is one HTML file. No build step, no dependencies, no network calls, no analytics — open one from a link or from your own disk and it behaves the same either way. Everything you type stays in your browser, and every setting lives in the page URL, so a copied link reproduces the exact scenario for whoever you send it to.

Australian FIRE Calculator

The earliest you could stop working, under Australian rules.

  • Two buckets, because Australia has two. Super is preserved until 60; everything outside it can be sold today. Retire before 60 and the years in between have to be funded entirely from outside super — the bridge, which is its own way for a plan to fail.
  • Real tax, not an average rate. The resident rate scale with Medicare, LITO and SAPTO; 15% on the way into super and Division 293 above $250,000; the 50% CGT discount when you sell; refundable franking credits, which in retirement can pay tax back.
  • The age pension from 67, estimated on both means tests with the lower payment winning, and the minimum drawdown that forces money out of super from 60.
  • A thousand other markets. The whole plan — tax, pension, drawdown — re-run against 1,000 return sequences, plus a solver for the earliest age that still holds.

Investment Growth Calculator

What contributions, returns, fees, tax and inflation do to a portfolio over time.

  • A lump sum and a habit. Contributions monthly or yearly, at the start or the end of the period, optionally rising with inflation, compounded at whichever frequency you actually get.
  • Fees and tax where they bite. A percentage fee on the balance the way a fund or platform charges it, and tax on earnings as they accrue — leave it at zero for a sheltered account.
  • The stack decomposes exactly. What you put in, what the returns paid, and what your returns went on to earn, which always sum to the balance — so the chart can be read as arithmetic rather than illustration.
  • Today's money against actual money, discounted year by year on its own date, with the return you would have needed at other rates for contrast.

Debt Recycling & Leverage Calculator

Borrowing to invest under Australian tax — and whether it actually left you ahead.

  • Three strategies, one engine. Debt recycling, which keeps the debt the same size and changes what it is doing; borrowing a lump against the house; and a margin loan, where the lender can force the sale.
  • Always measured against not borrowing. The same spare cash is spent either way and both sides see the same market every year, so the difference is the borrowing and nothing else. Set the borrowing to zero and the two ledgers agree to the cent.
  • The number that decides it: the break-even return. Found by bisection on the full projection. Below it the borrowing cost you money however good the story sounded.
  • Deductible interest, franking credits and the surcharge add-back — negative gearing cuts your income tax without moving you down a Medicare levy surcharge tier, which is the one place the deduction buys you nothing.

How they are built

  • One file, no toolchain. Markup, styles, maths and charts are all inline. There is nothing to install, nothing to compile, and nothing that can rot in a lockfile — file:// behaves exactly like https://.
  • Nothing leaves the page. No requests, no cookies, no tracking. State is kept in the URL hash and in localStorage, both of which live on your own machine.
  • Charts drawn by hand in SVG. No chart library. Colours are checked for contrast and colour-vision deficiency on both the light and the dark surface, and every series can wear a pattern as well as a colour.
  • Made to be read. Focus any chart and use the arrow keys; every result is also a table and a CSV; printing re-renders on a light ground.
These are educational tools, not financial, tax or superannuation advice. They take no account of your objectives, situation or needs, and nothing in them is a recommendation. Results are hypothetical illustrations generated from the figures you type, assuming steady average returns and unchanging law — real returns vary and may be negative, and tax and social security rules change often and apply differently to different people.
